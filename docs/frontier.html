The efficient frontier: do Greek hospitals have economies of scale?

Supplier spend per patient-day across 40 general hospitals, joined with official 2024 activity data. Short answer: no scale economies — but a clear frontier, with a recognizable profile.

No economies of scale

If bigger hospitals bought cheaper, cost per patient-day would fall with volume. It rises: the correlation between (log) annual patient-days and supplier €/patient-day is +0.30, and the volume tiers tell the story directly:

Energy shows the same non-result (correlation of beds with €/bed on energy: 0.15 — nothing). The likely driver is case-mix, not inefficiency: large hospitals practice tertiary medicine with expensive drugs. But the policy implication holds either way — this data offers no support for merger-for-scale arguments. Whatever big hospitals buy with size, it is not cheaper supplies per day of care.

The frontier — and its profile

Sorted by supplier cost per patient-day, a distinct efficient frontier emerges, and its members look alike: regional general hospitals of 230–400 beds running at high occupancy. Τρίπολη delivers a patient-day of care for €160 in supplier costs at 89% occupancy; Χαλκίδα combines €202/day with the country's #2 data-quality score — cheap and transparent are not in tension.

Drug prices: volume buys nothing — competition buys everything

To test scale economics at the product level, we parsed 100 supply contracts for high-cost oncology drugs at one hospital (2021–2023). Each contract conveniently prints the official hospital ceiling price (Δελτίο Τιμών) next to the negotiated one, so the discount is measurable per purchase. The result: patented immunotherapies (OPDIVO, PERJETA, TECENTRIQ, HERCEPTIN, IMFINZI…) sell at exactly the official list price — zero discount, across dozens of purchases and years. The only prices that move are where competition exists: the biosimilar MVASI's discount grew from 6.5% to 36.6% as the biosimilar market matured, and KEYTRUDA's early 14–19% discount vanished in late 2022 when its maker "changed commercial pricing policy" — recorded verbatim inside the contract. Quantity correlates only weakly with discount (0.34). In hospital purchasing, volume buys nothing; competition buys everything — the strongest argument in this dataset for accelerating biosimilar adoption and the weakest for consolidating purchase volumes.

What a real "north star" would take

The defensible target is not "the cheapest hospital" but a should-cost band per peer group at comparable occupancy: on this data, a well-run 250–400-bed regional general hospital at ~75% occupancy runs €130–200 per patient-day in supplier costs. Deviation above that band is not an accusation — it is a question with an address.

Two stated limits before anyone builds policy on it: this measures supplier spend only (payroll is excluded — though we demonstrated it can be extracted from each hospital's published απολογισμός), and there is no case-mix adjustment (Greece's ΚΕΝ/DRG data would be the natural next join). Within peer groups, both biases are muted; across groups they dominate — which is why this page compares only general hospitals.
