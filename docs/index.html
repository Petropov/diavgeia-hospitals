Greek public hospital payments, checked against the documents

An independent audit of the payment records 50 Greek public hospitals publish on Diavgeia, the state transparency platform. Three questions: what does the money look like, can the platform's data be trusted, and what does it reveal?

Part I

The overarching picture

Greece requires every public body to publish its spending decisions on Diavgeia. For hospitals that means, in principle, a public record of every payment order: who was paid, how much, and for what. We downloaded five years of these records (August 2021 – August 2026) from the 50 largest hospital publishers, classified hundreds of thousands of decisions, and — the step usually skipped — verified them against the scanned source documents.

The money

After corrections, the 50 hospitals paid €7.80 billion to third-party suppliers over five years through the main payment-order layer, growing steadily from €1.36bn (2022) to €1.68bn (2025) — about +24%, with no cliff or spike that would suggest a reporting-regime change. A further €244M flows through a second, structurally anonymous instrument (ΧΕ warrants). Pharmaceuticals and medical supplies dominate; the largest single verified payment is €6.58M for pharmaceuticals.

How complete is this picture?

For the hospital we reconciled line-by-line against its audited annual accounts, Diavgeia's payment layer captured ~91% of all third-party spending value — in both years tested. The missing ~9% is identifiable (the anonymous warrant layer), not lost. Diavgeia is, in short, a genuinely usable public ledger of hospital spending — with the reliability caveats that Part II measures.

How we know any of this

Every claim traces to a document: a stratified random audit of 300 payment records against their scanned warrants, verification of all 55 published payments over €5M, 22,199 procurement decisions pulled to resolve flagged patterns, and reconciliation against hospitals' own απολογισμός. All data, scripts, and verification PDFs are in the public repository.

Part II

The quality of Diavgeia

The data is 98% right…

In the 300-record random audit, 294 records matched their source documents exactly: a 2.00% error rate (95% CI 0.42–3.58%). Four of the six errors understated the real payment — the first direct measurement of an error class no automated screen can catch, because every screen hunts for numbers that are too big, never too small. The six errors net out to just +€250, so ordinary typos barely distort totals.

…but the errors that slip through are spectacular

We quarantined every published payment over €5 million: 55 records with a combined face value of €129 trillion — roughly 700 times Greek GDP. After checking each source document, exactly one was real. The rest decompose into five recurring failure modes:

Some stood uncorrected for years. A platform that cannot notice a €94 million accident cannot notice a €50,000 deliberate act — that asymmetry, not any individual typo, is the structural weakness.

The index predicts reality: in the random audit, the hospitals independently classified as clean produced zero errors in 120 documents; the known-worst produced the most. Several major hospitals publish zero budget codes, making machine oversight of what money was spent on impossible.

The loudest quality signal is silence

Crete's two largest hospitals — ΠΑΓΝΗ and Βενιζέλειο, under a joint administration — publish no payment records at all. In five recent months ΠΑΓΝΗ posted 3,732 decisions, including 1,450 procurement awards and commitments up to €11.8M — and zero payment orders. Procurement fully visible; money out, completely dark. Other Cretan hospitals publish normally: this is an institutional practice, not a technical one.

It exposes a perverse incentive in transparency measurement: bad data lowers your score, but no data exempts you from scoring entirely. Any ranking that silently drops non-reporters rewards the most opaque institutions. Our totals list these hospitals explicitly as "non-reporting at the payment layer".

Part III

Key findings

1. The structuring flags dissolved when the award documents were read

Payment patterns at one island hospital initially looked like textbook procurement structuring: three suppliers receiving repeated monthly payments just under the €30,000 direct-award ceiling. We read the award documents behind all three relationships. All three dissolved — each was a tendered, contracted service (catering, security/cleaning, electromechanical maintenance) invoiced monthly; one "supplier" was not even a company but the hospital ERP's label for a maintenance consortium's contract. Payment data alone would have convicted the innocent.

2. The real procurement risk is single-bidder capture, not threshold games

What the award documents revealed instead: on the island market, formally competitive tenders repeatedly attract exactly one bidder — and when larger open tenders are launched, competitor appeals stall them until the fallback procedure lands with the incumbent. A market-thinness problem, invisible in payment data, visible only in award documents.

3. Our corrections survive an external audit

For the hospital with the heaviest corrections, we reconciled a clean year and a ×100-contaminated year against its audited accounts:

YearOfficial third-party paymentsOur corrected seriesCoverage
2024 (clean)€21.5M€19.5M90.7%
2023 (×100-contaminated)≈€19.1M€17.5M91.6%

Identical ~91% coverage in both years — while the uncorrected 2023 series would exceed 100% of the official envelope, which is arithmetically impossible for a subset. The hospital's own audited accounts confirm the corrections. Category-level agreement: pharmaceuticals within 3–6%, cleaning exact, lab reagents within 1%.

4. Comparable hospitals cost the same — they just account differently

Two similar-sized hospitals we compared in depth (335 vs 318 beds) showed total payment value per bed within 1% of each other — while their published category breakdowns differed wildly, because one records budget codes on 99% of spend and the other, since 2026, on almost none. Cross-hospital benchmarking in Greece is possible, but only after correcting for how each hospital fills in the forms — which is itself a finding.

5. What should change

Validate at entry. Every error class found is machine-detectable at submission: an amount equal to the payer's tax ID, a 14-digit euro value, a cents-less number 100× the invoice trail — none should be publishable.

Score the publishers. A public per-institution quality index turns invisible negligence into a visible ranking — and demonstrably predicts document-level accuracy.

Close the silence loophole. Payment-layer publication should be reconciled against each hospital's απολογισμός — automatable, as we showed — and non-reporting institutions should appear as such in every official statistic.

Random audits, not outlier-chasing. Targeted screens only ever found overstatements; the understatements surfaced exclusively in the random sample. Randomized document verification is the only method that measures what you are not looking for.

The trend compares two-year averages — (2024+2025) vs (2022+2023) — because a single-year endpoint comparison is dominated by noise: one equipment purchase or arrears settlement flips the sign (measured: Καβάλα shows +53% endpoint-to-endpoint but −3% on averages). Two cautions remain even so: an apparent spending collapse can be a publishing collapse, and 2022 baselines carry COVID-era distortions. These are payment-record trends, verified only where stated.

What the table shows

Scale. Three Athens hospitals dominate: Ευαγγελισμός (€866M over five years), Αττικόν (€575M) and Λαϊκό (€530M). The top ten account for roughly €4.4bn — over half of all verified spend in the study.

Size does not buy quality. The correlation between spending volume and DDQI is weak: the largest spender ranks mid-table on data quality (58.7), and the best publishers are mid-sized regional hospitals (Μυτιλήνη 99.1, Χαλκίδα 97.4, Κομοτηνή 97.2). Eight hospitals — including Αττικόν, Νίκαια and Χανιά — publish effectively zero budget codes, so what their money buys cannot be machine-read at all.

Divergent trajectories. On the noise-robust averaged measure, the children's hospital Αγλαΐα Κυριακού grew +64% (from a small base) and ΚΑΤ +42%, while Ιπποκράτειο Αθηνών fell −37%. Λαϊκό — which looks like −20% on a naive endpoint comparison — is actually flat (−0.4%): its 2024 spike distorted both readings, a live demonstration of why the metric choice matters. Real drivers (post-COVID normalization, EKAPY centralization shifting purchases off hospital budgets) and recording changes are entangled; these are exactly the cases for the reconciliation method of Part III.

Concentration. We measure it as the top-5 suppliers' share of spend (CR5) — more robust than a single-supplier share, which one big contract can distort. The median hospital gives its five largest suppliers 23% of its money; the range runs from 13.4% (Γ. Παπανικολάου) to 49.8% (Παίδων Αγλαΐα Κυριακού, where five vendors take half). Small and specialty hospitals concentrate naturally — fewer, narrower needs. The cases worth an award-document look are those combining high CR5 with a dominant single supplier: Σωτηρία (CR5 40.7%, one supplier alone at 26.8%) and Κουτλιμπάνειο Λάρισας (40.3% / 28.2%).

Per-bed comparables (official 2024 bed & activity data)

Joining our verified 2024 spend with the Ministry of Health's official 2024 dataset of developed beds and patient days (moh.gov.gr) gives, for the first time, like-for-like supplier spend per bed per year across 45 hospitals (5 excluded for incomplete 2024 posting — comparing an incomplete ledger per bed produces nonsense like €205/bed, so we don't).

The spread is 16× — from €255,915 per bed per year (Λαϊκό) to €15,985 (Παίδων Αγλαΐα Κυριακού), median €62,268. Most of it is case-mix, not efficiency: the top of the table is transplant/oncology medicine (Λαϊκό's expensive hemophilia and transplant drugs, Αγ. Σάββας and Θεαγένειο oncology, Αττικόν tertiary care), the bottom is maternity (Έλενα €25,672) and pediatrics, where drugs are cheap. Per patient-day the median is €301, with Λαϊκό again top at €768.

The honest use of these numbers is within-peer-group comparison (use the filter above). The group benchmarks: large & university hospitals: median €119,782/bed/yr (range €73k Νίκαια – €256k Λαϊκό); medium general: €49,512 (€30k Κέρκυρα – €104k); small general: €56,612 (€38k Έδεσσα – €74k Γιαννιτσά); specialty: €78,372, with the widest spread because oncology and pediatrics are opposite cost worlds. Within the medium group the natural suspicion was Κέρκυρα: €30k/bed, 40% below its peer median, and last-place data quality — thrift or concealment? We resolved it the way Part III prescribes: its published ΑΠΟΛΟΓΙΣΜΟΣ ΕΞΟΔΩΝ 2024 (ΨΝΣΗ4690Β3-7Β0, cross-confirmed to the cent by its inquiry report) shows €19.72M total warrants, ≈€14.0M third-party — €34.6k/bed officially. Our dataset captures 87% of that, right at the Rhodes benchmark. Verdict: the low spending is real, not hidden; Κέρκυρα's bottom-place DDQI reflects sloppy field completion, not concealed money. The document also shows it executed only 57% of its €34.5M budget — a different question worth asking. This is what document-level verification is for: the same data pattern could have meant concealment, and only the reconciliation could tell.

Readings. Energy is the most universal and most comparable category — nearly every hospital buys from the same national suppliers, yet per-bed cost varies several-fold, a spread that heating degree-days and building age only partly explain; it is the single most promising target for pooled procurement. Αττικόν tops every outsourced-service category — it has externalized cleaning, catering and security at a scale no other hospital matches, spending ~€18k/bed/yr on the three combined; whether that buys better service than Ευαγγελισμός's €427/bed cleaning line is exactly the operational-efficiency question these data cannot answer but an inspectorate could. Nationwide suppliers — who sells to the whole system

Aggregating all 50 hospitals' verified payments by supplier tax ID (state counterparties like ΕΦΚΑ excluded — €331M of ministry/fund remittances were masquerading as "supplier" spend) maps the national hospital market: 10,535 distinct suppliers, €7.45bn. Sortable top-100 below.

What it shows. The single largest payee of the hospital system is not a pharmaceutical company but ΔΕΗ — electricity, €228M across 49 hospitals. Then comes big pharma (MSD €219M, Roche €180M+€96M diagnostics, Pfizer €121M, Gilead €101M) and medical devices (Medtronic €135M). 43 suppliers sell to all 50 hospitals — a genuinely national vendor layer; the widest-footprint domestic firm is a medical-devices company at €173M over 10,334 separate payments. Yet the top-10 suppliers hold only 20% of total value: nationally the market is dispersed, which makes the local single-bidder pockets documented in Part III stand out — competition exists in Greece; it fails to show up in specific places.

Method, limits, and reproducibility

Everything is reproducible from the public repository: raw API responses, classified datasets, every verification PDF, all scripts, and a sense-check log recording our own retracted claims — including a false 37.67% "error rate" produced by our own parser before we caught it. Known limits: totals are corrected for overstatement, with understatement bounded only by the audit CI; the anonymous warrant layer resists supplier attribution by design; hospitals below the top-50 are covered at 12 months; and the two non-reporting hospitals make any "national" total a floor, not a census.

Per our publication policy, suppliers are not named in findings; every underlying decision ID (ΑΔΑ) is public for independent verification.
